How loss of MYORG reshapes the astrocyte transcriptome

Question

A brain-calcification gene that lives in astrocytes

The disease

In PFBC, calcium phosphate builds up in the basal ganglia, thalamus and cerebellum, often around small blood vessels. Patients can develop movement disorders, psychiatric symptoms and cognitive decline.

The gene

MYORG encodes a glycosidase in the endoplasmic reticulum. In the brain it is made by astrocytes. Losing both copies causes the autosomal-recessive form of PFBC.

Why culture conditions matter

Serum pushes cultured astrocytes toward a reactive, injury-like state; serum-free medium keeps them closer to how they behave in the brain. Testing both shows whether MYORG loss depends on the cells' environment.

What changes inside astrocytes that lack Myorg, and does it depend on brain region and environment?

How to read an enrichment

Genes are ranked by sign(log2FC) × −log10(p). GSEA walks down the list and asks whether a pathway's genes pile up at the top (enriched, ▲) or the bottom (depleted, ▼). The drawing is a schematic, not data from this study.

Take-home

Without Myorg, serum-starved astrocytes turn on NF-κB inflammatory signaling, while cell-death and mitochondrial energy programs move in opposite directions in cortex and cerebellum: one mutation, region-specific astrocyte responses.
